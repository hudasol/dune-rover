### 14.2 Model families on the expanded curve set (SIMULATED BY ME against PUBLISHED curves; stages 21 and 22)

Rerun unchanged on the expanded set, no family passes the bar of 0.15: median held out error and pooled 90 % coverage are {{VAL:mv4_exp_existing_heldout}} and {{VAL:mv4_exp_existing_cover}} (exponential), {{VAL:mv4_mohr_coulomb_ratio_heldout}} and {{VAL:mv4_mohr_coulomb_ratio_cover}} (Mohr Coulomb), and {{VAL:mv4_logistic_empirical_heldout}} and {{VAL:mv4_logistic_empirical_cover}} (logistic). Against stage 15 the median held out errors fell by 0.02 to 0.03, and the exponential family's posterior moved its friction angle to the upper bound of its prior (median 44.3 against a bound of 45 degrees; stage 15: 36.9), so the friction angle is not identified by these curves. {{VAL:mv4_bw_sentence}} Holding out the two Scarab campaigns together (post hoc, because the Inotsume 2016 and Creager 2017 curves come from the same vehicle and tilt bed) gave {{VAL:mvg_heldout_min}} to {{VAL:mvg_heldout_max}}; also grouping the Yoshida 2010, Nakashima 2010 and Sutoh 2012 Toyoura sand studies as one laboratory (post hoc, after the final review) gave {{VAL:mvl_heldout_min}} to {{VAL:mvl_heldout_max}}. Two families built on the strength to load number of the granular scaling laws did worse than a curve with no covariates: {{VAL:mob_heldout_min}} to {{VAL:mob_heldout_max}} against {{VAL:mob_logistic_same}} for the logistic on the same curves. A post hoc check found that in the one study that varies only wheel width, wider wheels slipped more on the lunar simulant, the opposite of what the number implies. Three caveats weaken this test of the scaling laws: the narrow Watanabe wheels may not meet the plane flow condition the laws assume, the density parser takes the lower end of a reported range, and two soils without a reported density share the 1600 kg/m3 default. The families use 33 held out curves (34 loaded curves have wheel radius, width and load; the Dumont cubic fit is a target only and is not held out), and the logistic comparison uses the stage 21 logistic, which was calibrated on more curves. The scaling laws stay what their authors call them: similarity laws for geometrically similar wheels in one material, not predictors across vehicles and sands.

### 14.3 Simulator calibrated on a field curve (SIMULATED BY ME against a PUBLISHED curve; stage 24)

Project Chrono's Soil Contact Model, calibrated on the Dumont Dunes curve up to 12.5 degrees over a grid of the shear modulus and the friction angle, reached a calibration error of {{VAL:scm_cal_rmse}} (best K {{VAL:scm_best_K}} m, friction angle {{VAL:scm_best_phi}} degrees). Asked to predict 15 to 20 degrees, it gave {{VAL:scm_pred_15}}, {{VAL:scm_pred_17p5}} and {{VAL:scm_pred_20}} against {{VAL:scm_dumont_15}}, {{VAL:scm_dumont_17p5}} and {{VAL:scm_dumont_20}} on the curve (error {{VAL:scm_pred_rmse}}), with slip falling as the slope steepens. Post hoc grid and speed checks at those slopes (`results/chrono_scm_calibration/posthoc_checks.csv`) show the run is not numerically stable there: at 17.5 degrees a fourfold lower wheel speed gave {{VAL:scm_chk_speed_17p5}} against {{VAL:scm_chk_reg_17p5}}, and at 20 degrees a finer soil grid gave {{VAL:scm_chk_grid_20}} against {{VAL:scm_chk_reg_20}} while the lower speed made the wheel slide backwards (slip {{VAL:scm_chk_speed_20}}). At the calibration slopes (5 to 12.5 degrees) the same checks changed the slip by at most {{VAL:scm_chk_calmax}}, so the calibrated fit itself is numerically stable (post hoc). No usable prediction of the steep slopes was obtained; the stage 24 outcome measures a numerically unreliable run, not the physics of the Soil Contact Model. The particle (DEM) check is prepared as a notebook for a free GPU (`notebooks/chrono_dem_gpu.ipynb`) and has not been run.

### 14.6 Closed loop demonstration (SIMULATED BY ME on PUBLISHED lidar; stage 23 and a post hoc variant)

A simulated rover drove the stage 17 routes over the one metre lidar surface, with its true slip given by the exponential model at the stage 15 posterior medians. Its online slip estimate, from wheel odometry against position fixes with 0.3 m noise, had a median error of {{VAL:cl_est_WhiteSands}} (White Sands) and {{VAL:cl_est_Kelso}} (Kelso); that value follows almost by arithmetic from the assumed noise and exact odometry, and with 2 % odometry noise per metre it stayed at {{VAL:clph_est_WhiteSands}} and {{VAL:clph_est_Kelso}}. In the registered loop, stopping on a high estimate and planning again cut entrapment on 30 m naive routes from {{VAL:cl_WhiteSands_30_naive_open}} to {{VAL:cl_WhiteSands_30_naive_closed}} (White Sands) and from {{VAL:cl_Kelso_30_naive_open}} to {{VAL:cl_Kelso_30_naive_closed}} (Kelso), but the midpoint review showed this came mostly from restarting with fresh random slip. In a post hoc variant with the slip field fixed in space, the rover kept at its real position and a control that retries without blocking, one slip field gave on 30 m naive routes {{VAL:clph_WhiteSands_30_naive_open}} open loop, {{VAL:clph_WhiteSands_30_naive_retry}} retry and {{VAL:clph_WhiteSands_30_naive_replan}} replan at White Sands, and {{VAL:clph_Kelso_30_naive_open}}, {{VAL:clph_Kelso_30_naive_retry}} and {{VAL:clph_Kelso_30_naive_replan}} at Kelso. Against open loop, replanning raised entrapment on 10 m maps at Kelso by {{VAL:clm_Kelso_10_ro}} points (interval {{VAL:clm_Kelso_10_ro_ci}}), but retry alone raised it from {{VAL:clm_Kelso_10_open}} to {{VAL:clm_Kelso_10_retry}}, so most of that rise is the restart geometry described next, not the blocking of cells. The retry control is not neutral: after a stop, the route restarts with a straight line from the rover's real position to the next cell centre, so path geometry changes outcomes independently of slip feedback, which is why retry can do worse than open loop on fine maps. The same loop runs as ROS 2 nodes (`demo/ros2/`); one smoke test reached the same outcome as the Python loop ({{VAL:ros_outcome}} after {{VAL:ros_replans}} replan), on a pair that also arrives without replanning, with the random number order aligned to the Python loop; it shows that the nodes work together, not that the loops behave the same. These numbers describe the loop under its assumptions, not a vehicle: under them, slip feedback with replanning shows no benefit that can be told apart from stopping and retrying, and it is no substitute for a fine map.
