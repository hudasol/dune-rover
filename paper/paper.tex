\begin{abstract}
For most deserts the only free elevation models a small wheeled rover can plan on have a posting of about 30\,m, while the slip faces of active dunes are often only metres long. On six North American dune fields with one metre airborne lidar as truth, we scored hazard layers from slope and interpolation to boosted trees, convolutional networks, super resolution and imagery fusion against a lidar face map, and used them in a slip aware planner at operating points chosen on the other five sites. On 10\,m block means of the lidar the best layer raises mission success from 17\,\% to 64\,\%, and vehicle success (post hoc), which also requires every step to be climbable, from 9\,\% to 47\,\%. On 30\,m block means no computable layer adds more than 5.3 points to 8\,\% (at most 2.5 points of vehicle success), and interpolation on three real 30\,m products adds no more than 2.0 points. A face map oracle reaches 81\,\% on 30\,m maps, but only 17\,\% vehicle success. In exploratory closed loop runs with line of sight sensing, 30\,m priors raise success from 30\,\% to between 34\,\% and 48\,\%, and not clearly when the endpoints come from the coarse map. Code and the prediction log are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Small wheeled ground robots are proposed for survey, inspection and search in sand deserts, including the dune seas of the Arabian Peninsula. Their routes are planned on a prior map, and for most deserts the only free digital elevation models (DEMs) are global products with a posting of about 30\,m~\cite{copernicusdem,tadono2014aw3d,hawker2022fabdem}. The dominant hazard on active dunes is the slip face, the lee side of a dune held near the angle of repose of dry sand, about 30 to 34\,degrees~\cite{pye2009}, where wheel slip rises until a small vehicle can no longer climb~\cite{heverly2013,arvidson2017megaripple}. By count most face patches are shorter than one 30\,m cell, although they hold a small share of the steep ground (Section~\ref{sec:res}), so a 30\,m cell can hold a face while its mean slope is gentle (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}).

It is well established that a coarse grid understates slope~\cite{zhang1994}. It is not known how much this matters for a planner, which hazard layer to compute from a coarse map, how fine a map must be, and whether imagery, another product or onboard sensing changes the answer. We answer these questions on six North American dune fields where one metre airborne lidar provides the truth. The contributions are (1) a benchmark that scores hazard layers against a lidar face map whose meaning is the same at every resolution, on idealised surfaces from 2 to 60\,m, five real elevation products and imagery; (2) a comparison of twelve families of hazard layers, learned ones trained leave one site out; (3) a planning evaluation at held out operating points with two kinds of endpoints, scored by mission success and, post hoc, by vehicle success; and (4) an exploratory closed loop with line of sight sensing, and an analysis of detection against face length and grid spacing. Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists the notation.

\section{Related Work}
\textit{Overhead priors.} Airborne lidar and imagery at metre scale have served as route priors for ground vehicles~\cite{vandapel2006aerial}; drones can map ahead of the robot~\cite{delmerico2017active} or learn terrain properties from the air~\cite{fortin2025uav}, and planetary missions plan from orbital data~\cite{ono2016traversability}.

\textit{Slip on sand.} Geometric costmaps score slope and roughness on a grid~\cite{papadakis2013,goldberg2002}. On loose sand slip rises steeply with slope~\cite{ishigami2007,heverly2013,shrivastava2020}; slip can be learned from appearance~\cite{angelova2007slip}, desert vehicles have planned on overhead and onboard maps~\cite{thrun2006stanley}, and planners propagate slip uncertainty~\cite{inotsume2020robust}, assuming that the map resolves the slope.

\textit{Dunes in elevation models.} Dune morphometry from DEMs and imagery is well developed~\cite{hugenholtz2012remote,daynac2024workflow}, and super resolution can sharpen coarse DEMs~\cite{chen2016demsr}. Global 30\,m products have been ranked against lidar~\cite{guth2021ranking}; TanDEM-X, the radar behind GLO-30, is weaker over sand~\cite{martone2016enhancing}, AW3D30 comes from optical stereo~\cite{tadono2014aw3d}, and DEM error in a dune sea grows above 15\,degrees~\cite{wang2025multisource}. We know of no benchmark that scores coarse map hazard layers and planners for slip faces against lidar truth across resolutions.

\subsection{Sites, truth and label}
Six windows of one metre bare earth lidar from the 3D Elevation Program (3DEP) of the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS)~\cite{sugarbaker2014} cover active dune fields (Table~\ref{tab:sites}); the Dumont window also holds rocky hills, and three windows lie in off road vehicle areas. We chose the windows from 20\,m slope images to hold many faces, and had used them in earlier tests of the detector. A post is \emph{steep} when its Horn slope~\cite{horn1981} at 1\,m is $28^\circ$ or more, a little below the angle of repose of dry sand~\cite{pye2009}; voids never count as steep. A \emph{face patch} is an 8 connected group of at least 10 steep posts (10\,m$^2$). Face patches are steep ground, not verified slip faces. For a coarse cell $c$ with footprint $F_c$ the label is the face map
\begin{equation}
y_c = \begin{cases}1 & \text{if some } x \in F_c \text{ lies in a face patch},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\label{eq:label}
\end{equation}
which a planner needs; its meaning does not change with the cell size, although its prevalence does (Table~\ref{tab:sites}).

\subsection{Inputs}
\textit{Idealised:} block means of the lidar at 2 to 60\,m (I10 and I30 in the tables), with no product noise and no time gap. \textit{Real:} the USGS 1/3 arc second DEM (R10, about $8\times10$\,m)~\cite{usgs3dep13}, made from the truth lidar at all six sites (it tests the product chain only); NASADEM (N30, radar of February 2000)~\cite{nasadem}; Copernicus GLO-30 (G30, radar of 2011 to 2015)~\cite{copernicusdem}; and, post hoc, AW3D30 (A30)~\cite{tadono2014aw3d} and FABDEM (F30, GLO-30 with forests and buildings removed)~\cite{hawker2022fabdem}. R10, N30 and G30 are scored on their own geographic grids with labels from the lidar posts inside each native cell; A30 and F30, and G30 for planning, are interpolated to the 30\,m lidar grid; no shift is applied. \textit{Imagery:} one Sentinel-2 level 2A scene per site~\cite{drusch2012sentinel} (10\,m; lidar year, cloud below 5\,\%, lowest sun) and National Agriculture Imagery Program (NAIP) images~\cite{naip} (1\,m; dates: supplement S5).

\subsection{Hazard layers}
The layers (supplement Table~S2) are slope, ruggedness~\cite{riley1999}, plane roughness as in GESTALT~\cite{goldberg2002}, an aspect rule, interpolation (the steepest slope after cubic spline interpolation to 1\,m), learned super resolution (a dilated network~\cite{yu2016} that predicts a 5\,m residual), a logistic subgrid detector released with our planner and trained only on synthetic dune profiles, gradient boosted trees~\cite{friedman2001} in the histogram form of~\cite{ke2017} as implemented in scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011} on 14 terrain features, a dilated fully convolutional network~\cite{long2015fully} trained with Adam~\cite{kingma2015adam}, boosting on terrain plus Sentinel-2 or NAIP features, and, post hoc, a U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015unet} with early stopping and pixel level networks on the Sentinel-2 and NAIP bands. Every layer trained on lidar is trained leave one site out~\cite{roberts2017}, and a layer that is not a probability is calibrated by logistic regression~\cite{platt1999} on the other five sites when a planner needs one.

\subsection{Planner}\label{sec:planner}
The planner searches the 8 connected grid of the input. For an edge with along track grade $\tan\alpha$ (uphill positive) and cross slope $\tan\beta$,
\begin{equation}
d=\sqrt{(\tan\alpha+f_r)^2+\tan^2\beta},\qquad i=-i_0\ln\left(1-d/\tan\phi\right)
\label{eq:slip}
\end{equation}
give the traction demand and the slip ($i=\infty$ when $d\ge\tan\phi$), with friction angle $\phi$, motion resistance coefficient $f_r$ and slip scale $i_0$: the exponential shear law of Janosi and Hanamoto~\cite{janosi1961} for the ratio of demand to capacity. An edge is passable when its slip under the nominal parameters is at most $i_{\max}$ and costs $\ell/(1-i)$ for length $\ell$. Over 128 parameter draws, a cell is BLOCKED when it is passable (four of eight headings) in at most 5\,\% of draws, TRAVERSABLE when in at least 95\,\% and its face probability $p_c<\tau$, and UNKNOWN otherwise. The \emph{posterior vehicle} takes $\phi$, $f_r$ and $i_0$ from a posterior of Eq.~(\ref{eq:slip}) fitted by Markov chain Monte Carlo to twelve published slip curves of other wheeled vehicles (test rovers in Mars Yard sand and at Dumont Dunes~\cite{heverly2013}, Scarab and El Dorado~\cite{inotsume2016thesis}, Spirit~\cite{li2008spirit}; 5 to 95\,\% ranges $\phi$ 30.6 to $43.7^\circ$, $f_r$ 0.03 to 0.32, $i_0$ 0.33 to 1.21) and $i_{\max}$ from an assumed range of 0.4 to 0.7 (nominal $36.93^\circ$, 0.129, 0.627, 0.5; climbing limit 15.9$^\circ$). In a leave one study out test on 49 published curves no slip law met our bar (median held out root mean square slip error 0.15; best 0.20), so the vehicle is uncertain; a vehicle with narrower assumed ranges (limit 25.5$^\circ$) is the sensitivity. Three policies share the graph: \emph{slip only}, \emph{gate} (TRAVERSABLE cells only) and \emph{weight} (no BLOCKED cell, cost times $1+w\,p_c$ of the cell entered); gate and weight refuse descents of $28^\circ$ or more. Every layer is run at $\tau\in\{0.02,0.05,0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$ and $w\in\{1,2,5,10,20,50,100\}$ and reported at the setting with the highest mission success on the other five sites.

\subsection{Scoring and closed loop}
One hundred pairs per site, at least 1\,km apart, are drawn twice: E1 from 30\,m cells whose lidar posts are all below $20^\circ$ ($25^\circ$ at a site with fewer than 200 such cells; truth used) and E2 from cells whose coarse slope is below $15^\circ$ (no truth). A route, the polyline through its cell centres sampled every 0.5\,m on the lidar, is \emph{unsafe} when a sample falls on a face patch, and a \emph{mission success} when it exists and is not unsafe; \emph{vehicle success} (post hoc) also requires every 5\,m step to be passable for the nominal vehicle on the 5\,m lidar block mean, with no descent of $28^\circ$ or more. In the closed loop (20 pairs per site) the rover follows the plan on a 5\,m grid that inherits the coarse classes and face probabilities ($w=5$), with slip costs from the coarse surface interpolated to 5\,m. At every step it senses the face cells within $R=20$\,m, blocks them, frees the others and replans when its route is blocked, with up to 300 replans. In the main, post hoc runs a cell is sensed only when the line from an eye 1\,m above the rover's cell centre to a point 0.2\,m above the cell's centre clears the lidar at 19 equally spaced points; with missed detections each face cell is detectable with probability $p_d$, drawn once per pair.

\subsection{Statistics and prediction log}
Detection is scored by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with spatial block bootstrap intervals (300\,m blocks). The six sites are the units of generalisation: for planning we report the mean of the six per site differences with a $t$ interval (five degrees of freedom). Pairs are not independent (55\,\% to 82\,\% of the cells of a slip only route are also used by another route of its site), so we never test at the pair level. An exact sign flip test over six sites reaches at best $p=1/32$, which any correction for two or more comparisons lifts above 0.05, so we make no significance claims and read each gain by its size, its site level interval and the predictions logged before the runs. The detection results and mission success in Table~\ref{tab:planning} were designed in a prediction log with 43 numeric predictions before they ran (stages 25 to 28), but after earlier results on the same sites were known (31 hits, 12 misses; supplement S1). Vehicle success, the line of sight closed loop, A30, F30, the U-Net, the imagery networks and the face size checks are post hoc and exploratory (stages 29 to 31).


\section{Results}
\subsection{Which layer sees faces}
Table~\ref{tab:detection} gives the mean AUC of every layer for the face map label. On 10\,m block means most layers rank face cells well (interpolation 0.97, boosting 0.97, slope 0.90), and R10 behaves like the 10\,m block mean (interpolation 0.96). At 30\,m every elevation layer loses skill: with default settings interpolation is best (0.82), ahead of boosting (0.81) and the network (0.80), and the detector (0.74) is above only slope, the aspect rule and the NAIP features and network. Super resolution (0.84), the network trained four times longer (0.84), boosting with five more features (0.83) and the U-Net (0.83) do a little better.

Imagery adds a little. Boosting on terrain plus Sentinel-2 features reaches 0.85 at 30\,m (+0.04 over terrain alone), with NAIP 0.84; imagery alone is weaker (0.74 and 0.67; pixel level networks 0.78 and 0.62), and at 10\,m it adds nothing (0.97). Paired intervals put super resolution above interpolation at six sites and boosting with Sentinel-2 at five, by small margins (supplement Table~S4).

The real 30\,m products are worse than the 30\,m block mean: the best layer reaches 0.72 on GLO-30 and 0.63 on NASADEM on their own grids, and 0.73 on AW3D30 and 0.71 on FABDEM. Interpolation scores 0.712 on AW3D30, 0.678 on FABDEM and 0.696 on GLO-30,; the best shift raises GLO-30 only to 0.702, so misregistration does not explain the loss.

When a 30\,m cell needs 100 or 300 steep posts to be positive (fewer count as negative), interpolation scores 0.89 and 0.97, so cells with much steep ground are ranked well; other face rules change little (supplement S6).

\subsection{How fine a map must be}
\label{sec:res}
Fig.~\ref{fig:resolution}a shows how detection falls with grid spacing: interpolation scores 1.00 at 2\,m, 0.97 at 10\,m, 0.82 at 30\,m and 0.69 at 60\,m. Fig.~\ref{fig:resolution}b relates the fall to patch size. At every spacing boosting finds nearly all patches longer than four grid cells (96\,\% of patches 4 to 8 cells long at 30\,m), but at 30 and 60\,m far fewer patches shorter than half a cell (60\,\% at 30\,m, 46\,\% at 60\,m) (longer patches also touch more cells). By count most patches are short: 75\,\% are shorter than one 30\,m cell, but they hold only 7\,\% of the face posts.

\subsection{Planning}
Table~\ref{tab:planning} gives mission and vehicle success. The gate routes at most 34\,\% of pairs at 10\,m and 42\,\% at 30\,m: the slip model is uncertain on most dune slopes, so even gentle E1 endpoints are often not TRAVERSABLE on the coarse map (76\,\% of the refusals of boosting at 30\,m and $\tau=0.2$ are at an endpoint). The weight policy routes almost every pair and is chosen for every layer except slope and the detector on GLO-30, mostly at the largest weight on 10\,m maps; with weights up to 1000 (post hoc) success rises by up to +12 points at 10\,m and +1 at 30\,m.

On 10\,m maps every layer helps: mission success rises from 17\,\% with the slip model alone to 64\,\% with interpolation (+47 points, site level interval +22 to +73), and vehicle success from 9\,\% to 47\,\%. On 30\,m maps the gain is small: interpolation adds +5 mission points (interval +1 to +9) and boosting with Sentinel-2 +5 (0 to +10) to 8\,\%, but no computable layer adds more than 2.5 points of vehicle success, and slope, the detector and the network fall below the slip model alone: they flag much of the dune, so routes grow longer and still meet faces. Every layer trades length for exposure: the slip model alone drives 1.19 times the straight distance with 33\,m on faces per km, interpolation 1.37 times with 11\,m and the oracle 1.53 times with 2\,m. One contact fails a route, so cutting exposure by two thirds still leaves most routes failing. On GLO-30 no layer adds more than 1.7 points, on AW3D30 interpolation adds 2.0 and on FABDEM 0.2 (supplement Table~S13). The sensitivity vehicle and pair distance change little (Tables~S14 and~S10). With E2 endpoints the slip model alone reaches 3\,\% at 30\,m and the gain of interpolation is +3 (interval 0 to +6).

The face map oracle reaches 81\,\% mission success on the 30\,m grid but only 17\,\% vehicle success: 21\,\% of its successes are drivable, because its routes go round faces over slopes the coarse map shows as gentle. Registered degraded face maps at $w=5$ (supplement Table~S7) show how exact face information must be: the oracle gives 55\,\% at this weight, displaced by 10\,m 43\,\% and by 30\,m 11\,\%, and maps from simulated 2\,m and 5\,m surveys only 23\,\% and 8\,\%, because the 28\,degree rule misses faces on a coarser grid.

\textit{Face size.} Scored against faces of at least 60\,m only, 30\,m interpolation adds +8 points over the slip model alone (29\,\% to 37\,\%), against +42 on 10\,m maps; against patches of at least 300 posts it adds +13 (30\,\% to 43\,\%; supplement Table~S9). The 30\,m gap is smaller for large faces but remains. \textit{Stronger learned layers.} The U-Net adds +2 points at 30\,m (9\,\%; interval $-$1 to +4), and the NAIP network $-$1.

\subsection{Onboard sensing}
Table~\ref{tab:closedloop} adds onboard sensing within 20\,m (exploratory). A slip face lies beyond a brink, out of sight of an approaching rover (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}d), so a 5\,m cell is sensed only when in line of sight. With the slip model alone as 30\,m prior the rover succeeds on 30\,\% of the E1 pairs (65\,\% touch a face); the computable priors give 34\,\% (detector) to 48\,\% (interpolation, gain +18 points, site level interval +2 to +34) and the oracle 74\,\%. With E2 endpoints no computable prior has a site level interval above zero (best boosting, 38\,\% against 29\,\%). With the weight chosen on the other five sites instead of $w=5$, interpolation gives 53\,\% and the oracle 81\,\%. The sensor matters (supplement S6): with the slip model prior an eye at 0.5 or 1.5\,m gives 28\,\% and 42\,\%, and a detection probability of 0.8 drawn once per pair 12\,\%. On 10\,m maps interpolation and the oracle give 63\,\% and 82\,\% at $w=5$, below the open loop (64\,\% and 84\,\%), but 78\,\% and 85\,\% with the weight chosen on the other sites, so that gap came from the fixed weight.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\textit{What the results mean.} A 30\,m elevation model keeps enough of the dune shape to rank face cells, but not enough to plan around faces: one missed face fails a route of a kilometre, so recall, not ranking, decides planning, and the gap to 10\,m maps remains even for long faces. A 30\,m map also hides the steep climbs between faces, so even the face map oracle drives few routes the vehicle can climb. In the exploratory closed loop a coarse prior helps with E1 endpoints but not clearly with E2 endpoints, and the result moves with the sensor. An exact face map more than doubles success, but no computable layer approaches it, and survey face maps lose most of its value unless the face rule is recalibrated.

\textit{Limitations.} The truth is steep ground: rock, ruts and vegetated mounds also exceed 28\,degrees, three windows are off road vehicle areas, and the audit of 600 patches is not finished, so label precision is unknown. We chose the windows from slope images and had used them before, so earlier design choices may fit these sites; the prediction log is in our own repository, and much of the analysis is post hoc. The vehicle is a posterior fitted to slip curves of other vehicles and no slip law passed our transfer test, so absolute outcomes are uncertain. No rover was driven, the sensor model is simple, and the 10\,m results use lidar block means. Six sites are few: the site level intervals have five degrees of freedom, one unusual site such as Dumont can move a mean by several points, and the exact percentages are bounded by this sample. All sites are in the United States; on Arabian fields a megadune face 100\,m high spans about five 30\,m cells, but smaller flank faces would not.

\section{Conclusion}
A 30\,m elevation model ranks slip faces better than chance, but a planner using it does not keep a small rover off them. On six lidar dune fields no hazard layer computable from such a map, including imagery fusion, super resolution and a U-Net, added more than 5.3 points of mission success at its held out operating point, and real 30\,m products did worse, while interpolation on a 10\,m map raised it from 17\,\% to 64\,\%; the gap remains even for long faces and when the vehicle must also climb every step. In exploratory runs with line of sight sensing a 30\,m prior helped by +4 to +18 points. Next are the label audit, Arabian fields with a drone survey, and a rover with measured slip on the same sand.
