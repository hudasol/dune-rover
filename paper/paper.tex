\begin{abstract}
For most deserts the only free elevation model a small wheeled rover can plan on has a posting of about 30\,m, while the main hazard on active dunes, the slip face, is often only metres long. We built a benchmark on six North American dune fields, with one metre airborne lidar as truth, to measure what coarse maps hide and what a planner can do about it. Ten families of hazard layers, from slope and interpolation to boosted trees, a convolutional network, learned super resolution and imagery fusion, were scored against a lidar face map on 10 and 30\,m lidar block means (four of them on block means from 2 to 60\,m), on three real elevation products and with Sentinel-2 and NAIP imagery, and were then used by a slip aware planner at operating points chosen on held out sites. At 10\,m the best layer raises mission success from 17\,\% to 64\,\%. At 30\,m no layer we planned with adds more than 5 points to 8\,\%, because most faces are shorter than one cell. A face map from fine data would reach 81\,\% on the same grid, but a position error of 10\,m already removes much of that gain, and onboard sensing within 20\,m keeps the rover off faces only when it can see them. Code, results and the prediction log are in a public repository.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Small wheeled ground robots are proposed for survey, inspection and search in sand deserts, including the dune seas of the Arabian Peninsula. Before such a robot drives, its route is planned on a prior map, and for most deserts the only free elevation model is a global product with a posting of about 30\,m~\cite{copernicusdem}. The dominant hazard on active dunes is the slip face, the lee side of a dune held near the angle of repose of dry sand, about 30 to 34\,degrees~\cite{pye2009}, where wheel slip rises until a small vehicle can no longer climb~\cite{heverly2013,arvidson2017megaripple}. Many slip faces are shorter than 30\,m (Section~\ref{sec:res}), so a 30\,m cell can hold one while its mean slope is gentle.

That a coarse grid understates slope is long known~\cite{zhang1994}. What is not known is how much this matters for a planner, which hazard layer a practitioner should compute from a coarse map, how fine a map must be, and whether imagery, a different product or onboard sensing change the answer. This paper answers these questions with a benchmark on six North American dune fields where one metre airborne lidar provides the truth.

The contributions are:
(1) a benchmark that scores hazard layers against a lidar face map whose meaning is the same at every resolution, on idealised surfaces from 2 to 60\,m, on three real elevation products on their own grids, and with Sentinel-2 and NAIP imagery;
(2) a comparison of ten families of hazard layers, from slope and interpolation to boosted trees, a convolutional network, learned super resolution and imagery fusion, with every learned layer trained leave one site out;
(3) a planning evaluation in which each planned layer is used at the operating point chosen on the other sites, routes are scored on the lidar, the face map oracle is degraded by position errors and simulated surveys, and a closed loop adds onboard sensing; and
(4) a scaling result, detection skill against face length over grid spacing, that relates the survey resolution a field needs to the size of its faces.

\section{Related Work}
\textit{Overhead priors.} Vandapel et al.~\cite{vandapel2006aerial} planned ground vehicle routes on airborne lidar collected before the mission, and overhead imagery or lidar at metre scale has served as a cost prior since~\cite{sofman2006}. Recent work sends a drone ahead to map only what the ground robot needs~\cite{delmerico2017active} or learns terrain properties from a hovering drone~\cite{fortin2025uav}. Planetary missions plan from orbital data: traversability analysis selected the Mars 2020 landing site~\cite{ono2016traversability}, and slip predicted from orbital data drives route plans~\cite{hedrick2021terrain}. These priors are at metre scale; we ask what a 10 to 60\,m prior, the only one available for most deserts, hides.

\textit{Slip on sand.} Geometric costmaps score slope and roughness on a grid~\cite{papadakis2013,borges2022,goldberg2002}, and recent reviews cover traversability risk~\cite{benrabah2024review}. On loose sand slip rises steeply with slope~\cite{ishigami2007,heverly2013}; Curiosity met high slip crossing megaripples~\cite{arvidson2017megaripple}, and robophysical studies show how slope and granular flow interact~\cite{shrivastava2020}. Planners propagate slip uncertainty into routes~\cite{inotsume2020robust,endo2023riskaware,ono2015}, but they assume that the map resolves the slope, which fails on dunes at 30\,m.

\textit{Dunes in elevation models.} Coarser grids understate slopes~\cite{zhang1994}. Dune morphometry from DEMs and imagery is well developed~\cite{hugenholtz2012remote}, and deep learning maps crestlines from DEM derivatives~\cite{daynac2024workflow}. Global 30\,m products have been ranked against lidar and laser altimetry~\cite{guth2021ranking}; TanDEM-X, the source of GLO-30, performs worse over sand~\cite{martone2016enhancing}, and the error of open DEMs in a dune sea grows on slopes above 15\,degrees~\cite{wang2025multisource}. Learned super resolution can sharpen coarse DEMs~\cite{chen2016demsr,han2023global}. We know of no benchmark that scores coarse map hazard layers and planners for slip faces against lidar truth across resolutions.

\subsection{Sites, truth and label}
Six windows of USGS 3DEP one metre bare earth lidar~\cite{sugarbaker2014} cover active dune fields (Table~\ref{tab:sites}); we chose them from 20\,m slope images and had used them in earlier tests of the detector. A post is \emph{steep} when its Horn slope~\cite{horn1981} at 1\,m is $28^\circ$ or more, a little below the angle of repose of dry sand~\cite{pye2009}; voids never count as steep. A \emph{face patch} is an 8 connected group of at least 10 steep posts (10\,m$^2$). Face patches are steep ground, not verified slip faces. For a coarse cell $c$ with footprint $F_c$ the label is the face map
\begin{equation}
y_c = \begin{cases}1 & \text{if some } x \in F_c \text{ lies in a face patch},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\label{eq:label}
\end{equation}
which a planner needs and whose meaning does not change with the cell size. The cell label of the first version of this benchmark, at least 10 steep posts in $F_c$, means 10\,\% of a 10\,m cell but 1.1\,\% of a 30\,m cell and is reported only for comparison.

\subsection{Inputs}
\textit{Idealised:} block means of the lidar at 2, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30 and 60\,m (I10 and I30 in the tables), which isolate resolution from product noise, time and registration. \textit{Real:} the USGS 1/3 arc second DEM (R10, about $8\times10$\,m)~\cite{usgs3dep13}, NASADEM (N30, February 2000)~\cite{nasadem} and Copernicus GLO-30 (G30, 2011 to 2015)~\cite{copernicusdem}, each scored on its own geographic grid, with labels from the lidar posts inside each native cell; no horizontal shift is applied. For planning, GLO-30 is interpolated to the 30\,m lidar grid. Over these sites R10 is probably resampled by USGS from the same lidar collections. \textit{Imagery:} one Sentinel-2 L2A scene per site~\cite{drusch2012sentinel} (10\,m; lidar year, cloud below 5\,\%, lowest sun, chosen by rule) and NAIP~\cite{naip} (0.3 to 0.6\,m, resampled to 1\,m).

\subsection{Hazard layers}
Table~\ref{tab:methods} lists the layers: what a practitioner would compute from a coarse map, learned models, imagery fusion, and a logistic subgrid detector trained only on synthetic dune profiles, which ships with the planner and is treated here as one baseline among others. Every learned layer is trained leave one site out~\cite{roberts2017}, so the scored site is never seen in training.

\subsection{Planner}
The planner searches the 8 connected grid of the input. For an edge with along track grade $\tan\alpha$ (uphill positive) and cross slope $\tan\beta$,
\begin{equation}
d=\sqrt{(\tan\alpha+f_r)^2+\tan^2\beta},\qquad i=-i_0\ln\left(1-d/\tan\phi\right)
\label{eq:slip}
\end{equation}
give the traction demand and the slip ($i=\infty$ when $d\ge\tan\phi$), with friction angle $\phi$, motion resistance coefficient $f_r$ and slip scale $i_0$; the slip law is the exponential shear relation of terramechanics~\cite{wong2008} written for the ratio of demand to capacity. An edge is passable when $i\le i_{\max}$ and costs $\ell/(1-i)$ for length $\ell$. The four parameters are drawn 128 times; a cell is passable for a draw when at least four of its eight headings are, and $q_c$ is the share of draws for which it is. A cell is BLOCKED when $q_c\le0.05$, TRAVERSABLE when $q_c\ge0.95$ and its face probability $p_c<\tau$, and UNKNOWN otherwise. The main setting, the \emph{posterior vehicle}, draws the parameters from a posterior fitted by Markov chain Monte Carlo to published slip curves of other wheeled vehicles (5 to 95\,\% ranges: $\phi$ 30.6 to $43.7^\circ$, $f_r$ 0.03 to 0.32, $i_0$ 0.33 to 1.21; $i_{\max}$ 0.4 to 0.7; nominal climbing limit 15.9$^\circ$); a narrower assumed range (limit 25.5$^\circ$) is the sensitivity. Three policies share the graph: \emph{slip only}, \emph{gate} (TRAVERSABLE cells only) and \emph{weight} (no BLOCKED cell, cost times $1+w\,p_c$ of the cell entered); gate and weight refuse descents of $28^\circ$ or more. Every layer is run at $\tau\in\{0.02,\dots,0.9\}$ and $w\in\{1,\dots,100\}$ and reported at the setting that maximises mission success on the other five sites.

\subsection{Scoring and closed loop}
One hundred pairs per site, at least 1\,km apart, are drawn twice: E1 from cells whose lidar posts are all gentle (truth used, as in our earlier runs) and E2 from cells whose coarse slope is below $15^\circ$ (no truth). A route, the polyline through its cell centres sampled every 0.5\,m on the lidar, is \emph{unsafe} when a sample falls on a face patch, and a \emph{mission success} when it exists and is not unsafe. In the closed loop the rover follows the plan on a 5\,m grid that inherits the coarse classes and face probabilities ($w=5$), with slip costs from the coarse surface interpolated to 5\,m; at every step it senses the face patches within a radius $R$ from the lidar, blocks those cells, drops the face penalty of sensed free cells and replans when its route is blocked, within a window 300\,m around the pair and up to 300 replans.

\subsection{Statistics and prediction log}
AUC intervals come from a spatial block bootstrap~\cite{kunsch1989} with 300\,m blocks; route outcomes carry two stage bootstrap intervals (sites, then pairs). Site level claims use an exact sign flip test over the six sites, Holm corrected~\cite{holm1979}; the smallest attainable two sided $p$ is 1/32. On one core of a cloud x86 machine, the slip assessment of a 10\,km$^2$ window takes 0.2\,s at 30\,m and 2.3\,s at 10\,m, and planning 100 routes on it 0.2 and 2.2\,s. Designs and numeric predictions were committed to a prediction log before each run. Every result in Tables III to V was registered in this way, but after the results of the first version on the same sites were known; the checks in Section~\ref{sec:posthoc} are post hoc. The predictions of stages 25 to 28 (31 hits, 12 misses) and three amendments made before the affected results were known are in the supplement.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Which layer sees faces}
Table~\ref{tab:detection} gives the mean AUC of every layer for the face map label. On 10\,m block means most layers rank face cells well (interpolation 0.97, boosting 0.97, the detector 0.95, slope 0.90), and the real USGS 1/3 arc second product behaves almost like the 10\,m block mean (interpolation 0.96). At 30\,m every elevation layer loses skill. With the default settings of the learned layers, interpolation is best (0.82), followed by boosting (0.81), the network (0.80), roughness (0.77) and ruggedness (0.76); the detector (0.74) is above only slope (0.69), NAIP alone (0.67) and the aspect rule (0.61). The learned layers were held back by those settings: learned super resolution of the 30\,m surface reaches 0.84 and the network trained four times longer 0.84, the best elevation layers at 30\,m, and boosting with five more features, among them the interpolated slope itself, 0.83, so the interpolated slope carries information that the other terrain features lack. Tuning boosting by nested leave one site out changes nothing (0.81), nor does removing the detector's features (0.81). The information is still in the 10\,m data: the largest of the nine 10\,m interpolation scores in a 30\,m cell reaches 0.96.

Imagery adds information that elevation lacks. Boosting on terrain plus Sentinel-2 features reaches 0.85 at 30\,m (+0.04 over terrain alone), and with NAIP 0.84; imagery alone is weaker (0.74 and 0.67), and at 10\,m it adds nothing (0.97). Super resolution and boosting with Sentinel-2 are above interpolation at all six sites and the detector below it at all six, but with six sites none of the seven comparisons against interpolation that we tested at the site level (detector, boosting, network, super resolution, boosting with curvature features, with Sentinel-2 and with NAIP) is significant after Holm correction (sign flip $p=0.031$ at best, adjusted 0.22).

The real 30\,m products are worse than the 30\,m block mean: on their own grids the best layer reaches 0.72 on GLO-30 and 0.63 on NASADEM, which predates the lidar by about two decades; interpolating GLO-30 to the lidar grid changes little (best 0.72). Much of this loss is dune migration between the radar and lidar acquisitions rather than product error (Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}).

The label definition matters less than expected. With the earlier cell label (at least 10 steep posts) the 30\,m interpolation scores 0.82 against 0.82; requiring 100 or 300 steep posts raises it to 0.89 and 0.97, because large faces are easier to see; a 30\,degree threshold changes little (0.82). The labels are spatially correlated over 30 to 870\,m (the largest at Dumont, whose window holds rocky hills); with blocks of 870\,m instead of 300\,m the per site bootstrap intervals of slope and the detector still lie below interpolation at all six sites.

\subsection{How fine a map must be}
\label{sec:res}
Fig.~\ref{fig:resolution}a shows how detection falls with grid spacing. Every layer is almost perfect at 2\,m (interpolation 1.00) and still good at 10\,m (0.97); between 10 and 30\,m interpolation falls to 0.82 and at 60\,m to 0.69, while slope and the detector fall faster between 10 and 30\,m. Fig.~\ref{fig:resolution}b relates the fall to face size, with the length of a patch taken as the major axis of an ellipse with the same second moments as its posts. At every spacing, boosting finds nearly all faces longer than four grid cells (96\,\% of faces 4 to 8 cells long at 30\,m, 90\,\% at 60\,m), but far fewer faces shorter than half a cell (60\,\% at 30\,m, 46\,\% at 60\,m). Longer patches touch more cells and so have more chances to be flagged, which accounts for part of this rise. Most faces in these fields are short at 30\,m: 75\,\% of the patches are shorter than one 30\,m cell.

\subsection{Planning}
Table~\ref{tab:planning} gives mission success at the operating points chosen on the other five sites, and Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} shows the weight policy at every weight. With the posterior vehicle the gate routes at most 34\,\% of pairs at 10\,m and 42\,\% at 30\,m, whatever the layer, because the slip model is uncertain on most dune slopes; for every computable layer most refusals are at an endpoint (76\,\% of the unrouted pairs for boosting at 30\,m and $\tau=0.2$). The weight policy routes almost every pair and is chosen for every layer on the block means; on 10\,m maps the chosen weight is the largest in the grid ($w=100$) for most layers, so stronger weights may help further.

On 10\,m maps every layer helps. Mission success rises from 17\,\% with the slip model alone to 64\,\% with interpolation (+47 points, two stage interval +28 to +64, positive at all six sites), 59\,\% with boosting and 50\,\% with the detector. On 30\,m maps the gain is small: interpolation adds +5 points (interval +2 to +9) and boosting with Sentinel-2 +5 (interval +1 to +9) to 8\,\%, and no layer computed from GLO-30 has an interval above zero. With the assumed vehicle the 30\,m gain of interpolation is +7 points. Endpoints chosen without the truth (E2) lower every rate, the slip model alone to 3\,\% at 30\,m, and there the gain of interpolation is no longer clearly above zero (interval 0 to +6).

The face map, an upper bound that assumes face information from fine data, reaches 81\,\% on the 30\,m grid. The registered degraded face maps (Table~\ref{tab:planning}, lower part, at $w=5$; the gate routes too few pairs with this vehicle to compare) show how good that information must be: the face map gives 55\,\% at 30\,m, displaced by 5\,m 50\,\%, by 10\,m 43\,\% and by 30\,m 11\,\%. Face maps built with the same 28\,degree rule from simulated 2\,m and 5\,m surveys give only 23\,\% and 8\,\%; Section~\ref{sec:posthoc} shows why.

The outcome is robust to the scoring details. Counting a face within one post of the route (a vehicle footprint) raises the unsafe share of slip only routes at 30\,m from 92.5\,\% to 93.5\,\%; 2\,\% of the unsafe slip only routes touch faces only while descending the fall line, which some vehicles might survive; and the 28\,degree descent rule removes at most 0.05\,\% of the graph edges on the 30\,m grid, so it hardly acts there.

\subsection{Onboard sensing}
Table~\ref{tab:closedloop} adds onboard sensing that sees every face within the radius. With $R=20$\,m the rover reaches the goal without touching a face on 80\,\% of the pairs when its 30\,m prior holds only the slip model, and on 84\,\% with boosting as prior (+4 points, interval -1 to +13); the face map prior gives 85\,\%. With this idealised sensing no route between E1 endpoints touched a face; the failures are pairs for which the blocked cells of the prior and the sensed faces leave no way to the goal inside the 300\,m window around the pair. A better prior mainly saves replanning (29 replans per pair with the slip model prior, 8 with the face map), while the driven length grows slightly with it (1.16 against 1.19 times the straight distance). With E2 endpoints 5\,\% of the pairs touch a face whatever the prior, because some of these endpoints lie on or next to faces.

\subsection{Post hoc checks}\label{sec:posthoc}
These checks were decided after a second review of this paper and are post hoc; they use the operating points and pairs above.

\textit{Larger faces.} Scored only on face patches of at least 300 posts, the slip model alone succeeds on 30\,\% of the 30\,m routes and interpolation on 43\,\%; on 10\,m maps the values are 39\,\% and 93\,\%. Without Dumont, whose window holds rocky hills, the 30\,m values are 31\,\% and 47\,\%. The gap between 10 and 30\,m is therefore not only a matter of small steep patches.

\textit{Survey face maps.} The face maps from simulated surveys fail because the 28\,degree rule misses faces on a coarser grid: the 2\,m survey marks 59\,\% to 91\,\% of the face patch posts, depending on the site, and the 5\,m survey 8\,\% to 78\,\%, below 60\,\% at four sites.

\textit{GLO-30 and dune migration.} No shift was applied to GLO-30. At Dumont and Kelso, where it matches the lidar without shift, interpolation of GLO-30 on its own grid reaches an AUC of 0.85, against 0.62 at the other four sites, so dune migration since the TanDEM-X acquisitions, not only the posting, limits GLO-30. Planning on GLO-30 stays close to the slip model alone at both groups of sites (14\,\% with boosting against 10\,\% at Dumont and Kelso).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\textit{What the results mean.} A 30\,m elevation model keeps enough of the dune shape to rank face cells better than chance, but not enough to plan around faces: the best 30\,m layers add a few points of mission success, while the same layers on a 10\,m map add up to +47 points. Fig.~\ref{fig:resolution}b suggests why: faces several grid cells long are found at every spacing, and most faces in these fields are shorter than one 30\,m cell. Better learning helps detection a little (super resolution 0.84 against 0.82 for interpolation) but did not change planning (Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}). Imagery gave the largest gain in 30\,m detection, in line with the use of imagery in dune mapping~\cite{hugenholtz2012remote}, but in planning its gain was the same size as the interpolation's. The face map results put the remaining gap on information: face information from fine data, placed to within a few metres, would make most 30\,m routes safe, so a drone survey must be georeferenced accordingly and its face threshold calibrated for its own resolution.

\textit{Arabian dunes.} We did not test Arabian fields. A slip face 100\,m high at 32\,degrees spans about 160\,m downslope, five 30\,m cells, and usually more along the crest, so the large faces of Arabian megadunes should be visible at 30\,m, while the smaller faces on their flanks and on linear dunes would remain hidden. TanDEM-X, the source of GLO-30, performs worse over sandy deserts~\cite{martone2016enhancing}, and dunes migrate, so this is an expectation to test, not a result.

\textit{Limitations.} The truth is steep ground: rock, vehicle ruts and vegetated mounds also exceed 28\,degrees, Dumont, Algodones and Little Sahara are off road vehicle areas, and the manual audit of a stratified sample of 600 patches is not finished, so label precision is unknown. The windows were chosen by us and used in earlier tests of the detector, and the prediction log is kept in our own repository rather than a public registry. The vehicle model is a posterior fitted to published slip curves of other vehicles; in our own leave one study out validation no slip model family transferred between published studies (median held out slip error 0.20 to 0.41 depending on the family), so absolute route outcomes are uncertain. No rover was driven and no physics was simulated. Six sites limit the training data of the learned layers and the power of site level tests (the smallest attainable two sided $p$ is 1/32).

\section{Conclusion}
A 30\,m elevation model ranks slip faces better than chance but cannot keep a small rover off them. On six lidar dune fields no hazard layer computed from such a map, learned or not, with or without imagery, added more than a few points of mission success, while the same layers on a 10\,m map raised it from 17\,\% to as much as 64\,\%. Faces shorter than about four grid cells are the problem, so the survey resolution a rover needs depends on the size of the faces in its field. In simulation, onboard sensing within 20\,m kept every route between gentle endpoints off faces, and a better prior mainly saved replanning. The next steps are the manual audit of the face labels, the same benchmark on Arabian dune fields with a drone survey, and a rover whose slip has been measured on the same sand.
