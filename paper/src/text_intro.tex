\section{Introduction}
Small wheeled ground robots are proposed for survey, inspection and search in sand deserts, including the dune seas of the Arabian Peninsula. Their routes are planned on a prior map, and for most deserts the only free digital elevation models (DEMs) are global products with a posting of about 30\,m~\cite{copernicusdem,tadono2014aw3d,hawker2022fabdem}. The dominant hazard on active dunes is the slip face, the lee side of a dune held near the angle of repose of dry sand, about 30 to 34\,degrees~\cite{pye2009}, where wheel slip rises until a small vehicle can no longer climb~\cite{heverly2013,arvidson2017megaripple}. By count most face patches are shorter than one 30\,m cell, although they hold a small share of the steep ground (Section~\ref{sec:res}), so a 30\,m cell can hold a face while its mean slope is gentle (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}).

That a coarse grid understates slope is long known~\cite{zhang1994}. Not known is how much this matters for a planner, which hazard layer to compute from a coarse map, how fine a map must be, and whether imagery, another product or onboard sensing changes the answer. We answer these questions on six North American dune fields where one metre airborne lidar provides the truth. The contributions are (1) a benchmark that scores hazard layers against a lidar face map whose meaning is the same at every resolution, on idealised surfaces from 2 to 60\,m, five real elevation products and imagery; (2) a comparison of twelve families of hazard layers, learned ones trained leave one site out; (3) a planning evaluation at held out operating points with two kinds of endpoints, scored by mission success and, post hoc, by vehicle success; and (4) an exploratory closed loop with line of sight sensing, and an analysis of detection against face length and grid spacing.
