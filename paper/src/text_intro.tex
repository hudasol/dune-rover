\section{Introduction}
Small wheeled ground robots are proposed for survey, inspection and search in sand deserts, including the dune seas of the Arabian Peninsula. Before such a robot drives, its route is planned on a prior map, and for most deserts the only free elevation model is a global product with a posting of about 30\,m~\cite{copernicusdem}. The dominant hazard on active dunes is the slip face, the lee side of a dune held near the angle of repose of dry sand, about 30 to 34\,degrees~\cite{pye2009}, where wheel slip rises until a small vehicle can no longer climb~\cite{heverly2013,arvidson2017megaripple}. Many slip faces are shorter than 30\,m (Section~\ref{sec:res}), so a 30\,m cell can hold one while its mean slope is gentle (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}).

That a coarse grid understates slope is long known~\cite{zhang1994}. What is not known is how much this matters for a planner, which hazard layer a practitioner should compute from a coarse map, how fine a map must be, and whether imagery, a different product or onboard sensing change the answer. This paper answers these questions with a benchmark on six North American dune fields where one metre airborne lidar provides the truth.

The contributions are:
(1) a benchmark that scores hazard layers against a lidar face map whose meaning is the same at every resolution, on surfaces from 2 to 60\,m, on three real elevation products and with Sentinel-2 and NAIP imagery;
(2) a comparison of ten families of hazard layers, every learned layer trained leave one site out;
(3) a planning evaluation at operating points chosen on the other sites, with degraded face maps and a closed loop with onboard sensing; and
(4) a scaling result, detection skill against face length over grid spacing.
