\textit{What the results mean.} At 10\,m the face hazard is mostly visible and the choice of method matters little; at 30\,m it is mostly invisible, and no model of the coarse surface, physical or learned, recovers enough of it to plan face free routes, although the better layers halve the length of route on faces. The post hoc oracle locates the limit: a face map made from fine data makes even a 30\,m planning grid safe, so the missing ingredient is face information, not planning resolution. At 30\,m the cubic interpolation, which adds no information to the coarse surface, ranks face cells as well as or better than models trained on lidar of five other fields; we suspect that its steepest interpolated slope summarises local curvature more sharply than the features we gave the learned models, but we have not tested this. Our synthetic detector, trained on two dimensional straight crested profiles, ranks worse than both and underestimates how common faces are; its remaining value is that it needs no lidar and gives a probability that a recalibration on any surveyed field can correct. For a planner, the lesson is to treat a 30\,m elevation model as a coarse prior that cannot certify ground as traversable (the released planner already refuses to call such ground verified), and to obtain a face map from metre scale data, such as a drone survey, before driving, or to rely on onboard sensing.

\textit{Limitations.} The six fields are North American; the linear, star and compound megadunes of the Rub' al Khali are larger and only Great Sand Dunes holds star dunes, so the numbers transfer to Arabia as an indication only. The truth counts every surface steeper than 28\,degrees, including rock and lidar artefacts at Dumont; the secondary label limited to 36\,degrees changes little. The registered planning outcome is strict: any contact with a face patch of 10\,m$^2$ counts as unsafe, although some contacts would be short or survivable for a given vehicle, and a real rover would follow the route with a local planner and its own sensors; the post hoc exposure and patch size analyses show how the absolute numbers depend on this choice while the ranking does not. Route outcomes of one site share terrain, so we report intervals that resample sites as well as pairs; the exact McNemar tests in the repository treat pairs as independent and are optimistic. The vehicle model is an assumption range, not a measured rover, and no route was driven. GLO-30 differs from the lidar in sensor, date and datum, so the G30 results mix dune migration with product error. Six sites limit the leave one site out training of the learned baselines, and no hyperparameter was tuned for any method.
