Small wheeled robots that cross sand dunes usually plan on a global 30\,m elevation model, but the main hazard on active dunes, the slip face, is often only metres long. We built a benchmark on six North American dune fields, with one metre airborne lidar as truth, to measure what coarse maps hide and what a planner can do about it. Ten families of hazard layers, from slope and interpolation to boosted trees, a convolutional network, learned super resolution and imagery fusion, were scored against a lidar face map on lidar block means from 2 to 60\,m, on three real elevation products and with Sentinel-2 and NAIP imagery, and were then used by a slip aware planner at operating points chosen on held out sites. At 30\,m no layer, including imagery fusion, adds more than {{VAL:ho_post_E1_I30_DEMplusS2_d}} points to {{VAL:nv_post_E1_I30}}, because most faces are shorter than one cell; a face map from fine data would reach {{VAL:ho_post_E1_I30_FACEMAP}} on the same grid if placed within about 5\,m, and onboard sensing within 20\,m keeps the rover off faces whatever the prior, although some pairs then have no way through. Code, results and the prediction log are in a public repository.
