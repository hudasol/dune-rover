For most deserts the only free elevation model a small wheeled rover can plan on has a posting of about 30\,m, while the main hazard on active dunes, the slip face, is often only metres long. We built a benchmark on six North American dune fields, with one metre airborne lidar as truth, to measure what coarse maps hide and what a planner can do about it. Ten families of hazard layers, from slope and interpolation to boosted trees, a convolutional network, learned super resolution and imagery fusion, were scored against a lidar face map on 10 and 30\,m lidar block means (four of them from 2 to 60\,m) and on three real elevation products, and were used by a slip aware planner at operating points chosen on held out sites. At 10\,m the best layer raises mission success from {{VAL:nv_post_E1_I10}} to {{VAL:ho_post_E1_I10_BICUBICcal}}; at 30\,m no layer we planned with adds more than {{VAL:ho_post_E1_I30_DEMplusS2_dabs}} points to {{VAL:nv_post_E1_I30}}, because most faces are shorter than one cell. A face map from fine data would reach {{VAL:ho_post_E1_I30_FACEMAP}} on the 30\,m grid, and onboard sensing that sees every face within 20\,m keeps the rover off faces whatever the prior. In post hoc checks, the face map fell from {{VAL:pc_I30_FACEMAP}} to {{VAL:pc_I30_shift10}} when displaced by 10\,m, and with line of sight the rover succeeded on {{VAL:los_none}} of the pairs with the slip model prior and {{VAL:los_FACEMAP}} with a face map prior. Code, results and the prediction log are public.
