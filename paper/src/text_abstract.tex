For most deserts the only free elevation model a small wheeled rover can plan on has a posting of about 30\,m, while the main hazard on active dunes, the slip face, is often only metres long. We built a benchmark on six North American dune fields, with one metre airborne lidar as truth, to measure what coarse maps hide and what a planner can do about it. Ten families of hazard layers, from slope and interpolation to boosted trees, a convolutional network, learned super resolution and imagery fusion, were scored against a lidar face map on 10 and 30\,m lidar block means (four of them on block means from 2 to 60\,m), on three real elevation products and with Sentinel-2 and NAIP imagery, and were then used by a slip aware planner at operating points chosen on held out sites. At 10\,m the best layer raises mission success from {{VAL:nv_post_E1_I10}} to {{VAL:ho_post_E1_I10_BICUBICcal}}. At 30\,m no layer we planned with adds more than {{VAL:ho_post_E1_I30_DEMplusS2_dabs}} points to {{VAL:nv_post_E1_I30}}, because most faces are shorter than one cell. A face map from fine data would reach {{VAL:ho_post_E1_I30_FACEMAP}} on the same grid, but a position error of 10\,m already removes much of that gain, and onboard sensing within 20\,m keeps the rover off faces only when it can see them. Code, results and the prediction log are in a public repository.
