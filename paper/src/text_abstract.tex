Small wheeled robots that cross sand dunes usually plan on a global 30\,m elevation model, but the main hazard on active dunes, the slip face at the angle of repose, is often metres to tens of metres long. We measure what such maps hide with a preregistered benchmark on six North American dune fields, using one metre airborne lidar as truth and three coarse inputs: 10\,m and 30\,m block means of the lidar and the real Copernicus GLO-30 product. A subgrid slip face detector trained only on synthetic dune profiles is compared with slope, ruggedness, roughness and interpolation baselines and with gradient boosted trees and a convolutional network trained leave one site out on lidar labels; a released planner is then run with each hazard layer and its routes are scored on face patches in the lidar. At 10\,m every method except roughness ranks face cells well (mean AUC {{VAL:auc_I10_SLOPE_mean}} to {{VAL:auc_I10_GBM_mean}}). At 30\,m a cubic interpolation of the coarse surface is best or tied best (mean AUC {{VAL:auc_I30_BICUBIC_mean}}), the learned models follow, and the synthetic detector ({{VAL:auc_I30_DET_mean}}) beats only plain slope; on GLO-30 all methods fall to {{VAL:auc_G30_DET_mean}} to {{VAL:auc_G30_CNN_mean}}. In planning, hazard layers computable from a 30\,m map roughly halve the length of route on faces but lift mission success only from {{VAL:pl_assumed_I30_naive_none_mission_success}} to at most {{VAL:pl_assumed_I30_risk_weighted_BICUBICcal_mission_success}}; at 10\,m the best layer reaches {{VAL:pl_assumed_I10_risk_weighted_GBM_mission_success}}. A post hoc check shows that a perfect face map on the same 30\,m grid would reach {{VAL:ph_I30_conservative_ORACLEANY_success_p10}} with no unsafe route: the limit is the face information that coarse elevation does not hold, not the planning grid.
